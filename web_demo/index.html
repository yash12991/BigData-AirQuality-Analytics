Person 3: Distributed Machine Learning & Forecasting Lead

Track Focus: Spark MLlib Pipelines ➔ 24h Ahead Regression ➔ 6-Tier Hazard Classification
⏱ 6:00 – 9:00 (3 Minutes)
Say this for your MLlib defense:
"I implemented the distributed machine learning and forecasting pipeline using Spark MLlib. To ensure genuine scientific validity, we instituted three rigorous safeguards: First, zero data leakage via a strict chronological 80/20 train/test block split—10,294 historical observations for training and 2,570 future observations held out for testing. Second, we established a Naive Persistence Baseline (tomorrow's pollution equals today's: ŷ_{t+24} = y_t), which yielded an RMSE of 42.16 µg/m³. Third, we engineered multi-scale temporal lag features (t-1h to t-24h) and weather vectors using Spark's VectorAssembler. We trained distributed Linear Regression with ElasticNet (37.86 RMSE) and Gradient-Boosted Trees (37.61 RMSE). Our champion model—distributed Spark MLlib RandomForestRegressor—achieved an RMSE of 35.17 µg/m³, delivering a 16.6% error reduction over baseline with an R² of 0.306 in just 1.49 seconds. Furthermore, our RandomForestClassifier predicted the 6 CPCB health hazard categories with 70.21% test accuracy and a weighted F1-score of 0.676."

🛡️ Academic Evaluator Viva Defense Guide

        
Authoritative, rock-solid answers to the toughest questions project review committees ask during Track B evaluations.

      


      

        

          Q1: "Why did you use Apache Spark and Hadoop HDFS instead of Pandas and Scikit-Learn?"
          ▼
        

        

          Answer: "Single-node tools like Pandas load the entire dataset into a single JVM/memory space, creating an immediate out-of-memory bottleneck as telemetry scales to multi-year, multi-station sensor networks. 
          In our Track B architecture, Hadoop HDFS distributes raw data blocks (128 MB blocks with 3x replication), allowing Spark workers to execute parallel in-memory map-side partitions. Furthermore, Spark MLlib parallelizes decision-tree node splits and distributed ensemble evaluations across executor cores simultaneously, training complex ensembles in under 2 seconds."
        

      


      

        

          Q2: "How did you calculate AQI? Can't we just take the arithmetic average of all pollutant concentrations?"
          ▼
        

        

          Answer: "No, taking an arithmetic average of pollutant concentrations is scientifically and regulatorily invalid. Different air pollutants have completely different molecular toxicities—for instance, 60 µg/m³ of PM2.5 represents moderate air pollution, whereas 60 µg/m³ of SO2 is completely safe.
          We strictly implemented India's official Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) NAQI standard:
          1. Each pollutant's 24-hour or 8-hour concentration is converted into an independent sub-index via linear segmented interpolation using official CPCB breakpoints.
          2. The final AQI is determined by the maximum operator: AQI = max(I_1, I_2, ... I_n).
          3. We also enforced the CPCB rule requiring at least 16 hours of data in a 24-hour cycle and at least 3 pollutants, one of which must be PM2.5 or PM10."
        

      


      

        

          Q3: "Did you use K-Fold Cross-Validation for your ML model?"
          ▼
        

        

          Answer: "We specifically avoided random K-fold cross-validation because in time-series forecasting, random splits leak future observations into past training sets, causing artificially inflated accuracy that collapses in production.
          Instead, we implemented a strict chronological block split: the first 80% of historical records (10,294 samples) were used for training, and the final 20% strictly future horizon (2,570 samples) was held out for testing. This guarantees zero temporal data leakage."
        

      


      

        

          Q4: "Why did you compare your model against a Naive Persistence Baseline?"
          ▼
        

        

          Answer: "In atmospheric science, air pollution exhibits heavy autocorrelation: tomorrow's pollution often resembles today's pollution. Simply reporting an R² or RMSE in isolation without a baseline is misleading. In Track B, evaluation must be scientifically defensible.
          We established the persistence baseline (ŷ_{t+24} = y_t), which yielded an RMSE of 42.16 µg/m³. Our Spark MLlib Random Forest model beat this baseline by 16.6%, reducing RMSE to 35.17 µg/m³ and proving that our lag feature engineering extracted genuine meteorological and diurnal patterns."
        

      


      

        

          Q5: "How are your Parquet datasets partitioned in HDFS and why?"
          ▼
        

        

          Answer: "Our processed Parquet dataset in HDFS (/air_quality/processed/hourly_aqi_parquet/) is partitioned by city and year. This partitioning aligns directly with real-world query access patterns: when an analytics query or ML model trains on a specific city or year, Spark applies partition pruning, reading only the relevant directories from HDFS and avoiding expensive full-table scans."
        

      


      

        

          Q6: "How did you handle missing values in raw sensor streams?"
          ▼
        

        

          Answer: "We never substituted zero for missing values, as zero represents pristine unpolluted air and would catastrophically bias rolling averages downward. 
          Instead, we computed 24-hour and 8-hour rolling statistics requiring at least 16 valid readings per window. If fewer than 16 hours were available, the CPCB index was flagged as invalid (cpcb_is_valid = false). In the ML feature pipeline, Spark's VectorAssembler(handleInvalid='skip') dropped incomplete lag rows, ensuring only complete temporal histories entered the estimators."
