🔵 PERSON 2: Spark ETL & India CPCB Regulatory Lead ⏱ 3:00 – 6:00 (3 Minutes)
STEP 1: SAY THIS FIRST (Explain Spark ETL & CPCB Standards):
"Thank you, [Person 1]. Once the data sat in HDFS, I used Apache Spark 4.2.0 to clean it and calculate the official Indian Air Quality Index.

A major flaw in naive implementations is simply averaging raw pollutant concentrations. That is scientifically invalid: 60 of PM2.5 is moderate pollution, but 60 of SO2 is completely safe.

We strictly implemented India's official Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) methodology:
1. We harmonized all gases from ppb to mass concentrations (µg/m³).
2. We calculated 24-hour moving averages for particulates and 8-hour averages for CO and Ozone.
3. We enforced the strict 16-hour rule: a day is only valid if there are at least 16 hours of real sensor readings. We never replace missing numbers with zero.
4. We calculated sub-indices using CPCB linear breakpoints, and the highest sub-index becomes the final composite AQI."

To ensure our machine learning evaluation was genuinely sound and realistic, we did two critical things:
1. Zero Data Leakage: We did NOT use random K-Fold splits, because in time series, random splits cheat by leaking future weather into the past. We used a strict chronological 80/20 block split—training on the first 10,294 historical hours and testing on the final 2,570 future hours.

🛡️ Top 5 Viva Defense Questions & Answers (Who Answers What)
Q1: "Why use Hadoop HDFS and Apache Spark instead of Pandas and Scikit-Learn?" (Answered by Person 1)
Answer: "Pandas loads the entire dataset into a single computer's RAM, which crashes with out-of-memory errors as sensor telemetry grows to millions of rows. HDFS distributes data blocks across DataNodes, and Spark executes transformations and ML tree splits in parallel across executor cores in just 1.5 seconds."
Q2: "Can't we just calculate AQI by taking the average of all pollutant concentrations?" (Answered by Person 2)
Answer: "No, that is scientifically invalid. Different pollutants have completely different toxicities—for instance, 60 µg/m³ of PM2.5 is Moderate pollution, while 60 µg/m³ of SO2 is completely clean. CPCB requires piecewise linear sub-indices for each pollutant, with the final AQI determined by the maximum operator: AQI = max(I_1, I_2, ... I_n)."
Q3: "Did you use K-Fold Cross-Validation for your machine learning models?" (Answered by Person 3)
Answer: "No, because in time-series forecasting, random shuffling leaks future weather into past training sets. We implemented a strict chronological 80/20 block split: the first 80% historical timestamps for training, and the final 20% strictly future horizon for testing. This guarantees zero data leakage."
Q4: "Why did you benchmark against a Naive Persistence Baseline?" (Answered by Person 3)
Answer: "Air pollution has strong autocorrelation: tomorrow's pollution often resembles today's. Reporting an R² or RMSE without a baseline is misleading. Our Random Forest model beat the persistence baseline by 16.6%, proving that our lag feature engineering learned real atmospheric dynamics."
Q5: "How did you handle missing values in sensor streams?" (Answered by Person 2)
Answer: "We never imputed missing values with zero, because zero implies clean mountain air and would falsely drag averages down. Instead, we enforced CPCB's statutory 16-hour completeness rule: if a 24-hour window had fewer than 16 valid readings, the AQI was marked invalid."
